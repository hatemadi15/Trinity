# Earthrise — web app

Next.js 16 app for the CLPS Lunar Mission Browser (NASA Space Apps 2026, team Astroy/Ed).

## Setup

Node.js 22 (20.9 to 26 works; `.nvmrc` holds 22 for nvm and fnm), and npm run here, in `web/`. The root README's "Before you start: Node.js and npm" covers installing Node and what to do when npm fails.

    npm ci
    # copy the pipeline artifacts (run from pipeline/): uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli sync-local --tiles
    npm run dev

`.npmrc` sets `legacy-peer-deps=true` because npm 10's peer resolver crashes on vitest 5's peer set. `package.json`'s `engines` accepts Node 20.9 to 26, the versions a clean install, the tests and the build were checked on.

## Engine

`src/lib/engine/` is a pure-TypeScript port of the Python pipeline science (no DOM, no React):
time (UTC ↔ TDB), geometry (ENU az/el with parallax, south polar stereographic), ephemeris
(Catmull-Rom over the hourly MOON_ME table), tiles (zlib int16 DEM tiles, bilinear sampler),
horizon (nested-grid skyline with fallback bands), metrics (sunlit / Earth-visible series,
prefix-sum window index, best-window search).

Parity gates (all in `npm test`): JPL Horizons az/el < 0.002° (achieved 2.7e-5°), Python masks
< 0.01° (achieved 0.0005°, the JSON rounding quantum), metrics fractions < 1e-6 (exact).
Demo: `npm run engine:demo -- --site shackleton_center_rim_high` (mask in about 2 s cold, under 1.5 s warm in Node; `computeSiteMask` also reports DEM coverage and any tiles that failed to load).

## Routes

Every page keeps its first screen short (spec 20.1): explanations sit one tap away behind a button that says what it opens ("Why this happens", "How to read the chart", "About this view", "How these figures are counted", "How to read this", "Showing it to a class?"; `src/components/Fold.tsx`), and a test per page caps the words that show before a tap at 1440 and 390 px (`src/test/visible-words.ts`).

- `/`: home page. Opens with the arrival (see "The arrival" below), whose first screen says what the product does; with a proof line on its first screen ("Built on NASA LRO LOLA terrain and JPL DE440; checked against JPL Horizons to 0.00003° and against real landers.", with "See the checks") and "See why the pole is hard" (to `#why`); then charts one real month at de Gerlache Rim 2 (the Sun's and Earth's rows and what they mean for a small lander), explains why the south pole is hard in three drawings, shows a computed shadow picture of the ridge beside three real places (the ridge, and the Athena and Blue Ghost landers of March 2025, which open Explore at the first whole hour after each landed), and states the three checks against NASA. It closes with the ways on: compare sites, ask in plain words, print the brief, run it offline (`/learn#offline`).
- `/sites`: "Choose a landing site". Every catalogue site in three plain groups with its best landing day in the chosen period, its time with power from that day, and its sunlight and contact with Earth over the first N days after it (`loadOverview` in the store works them out one site at a time; places that never see Earth sit under their own heading), a relief map of the pole with one pin per site (names written where they fit, `src/lib/viz/maplabels.ts`), and a form that computes any other place south of 60° S. A row opens Explore on the landing date it names, where every figure of the row is one Explore shows.
- `/explore`: the answer for one site and period. The answer says how long the power lasts and why, and offers the landing date inside the period that gives the longest time with power ("Land on 20 January instead"); a link searches the next 12 months. Below it: the numbers, a chart of the Sun, Earth and the battery hour by hour on one time axis, the ground (one sentence for the time bar's hour: is the site lit, can Earth be reached; then three tabs: "Shadow map" with north up, "3D from above" (the same light on the terrain) and "Horizon chart" (the hills all round with the Sun's and Earth's paths); a site with no polar terrain opens on the horizon chart and shows the globe in 3D), where the site is with its nearest neighbours within 300 km, and the assistant; the time bar stays at the bottom of the window. `?site=&start=&days=` opens a site and period.
- `/explore/stand`: "Stand on the Moon", the full-window view from the site (`src/components/stand/`), opened by "Stand at the site" beside the ground panel's tabs and under the answer. `?site=&start=&days=&hour=` as Explore plus the hour; `?bright=1` for "Brighter picture"; `?quality=fast` for weaker graphics (pixel ratio 0.75, no drawn detail, coarser shadow steps). It reuses the store, the words (`src/lib/viz/stand.ts`: the status line on the lander's power and radio, the headline, the key moments and their captions, the Sun and Earth labels, the line on what is measured and what is drawn, and the reasons under "Why this happens") and the time bar, which carries the key moments as flags. Over the view the answer comes first: the headline on the status plate (its short form below 768 px), the next step under it, and from 768 px the lander's power and radio at the plate's foot with links to Earthrise, How it works and Accuracy; "More" opens today's card (on a phone the lander's state first, then the headline, what to watch, the better landing day, the reasons, "Brighter picture" for projectors and the links). On arrival one line over the view says "Press Play to watch the month go by · drag to look around" until the first turn, Play or flag; today's card shows what to watch and how to drag once it has gone. A small Easter egg stands opposite the lander, out of the opening view (`standResident` in `src/lib/viz/standOpening.ts`); it shares a boulder's geometry and material, so it adds no shader program, and it is off in the arrival, in demo mode and with `?bright=1`. The row of buttons ("Turn to: Sun, Earth, Lander", "Look up", "Show paths", "Hide the words") sits at the top right from 1280 px wide and above the time bar below that. Only the Sun, Earth and the lander are named until "Show paths", which shows the period's Sun and Earth paths (marked with the days of sunset, sunrise and Earth rising and setting), the level horizon where it clears the hills, the star names at night and your shadow's length. "Hide the words" leaves a bare time bar. Without WebGL 2 it shows the horizon chart. The development laptop's integrated graphics are not a target; the demo runs on a laptop with a discrete GPU. From 768 px wide the status plate carries the next step under its line (parts D2 and D3): the better landing day with its power and radio as the one gold action (else Compare sites), then one "Change ▾" holding Change site, Change date, Change lander (the forecast page's lander dialog, in place) and Compare sites (part F); on a phone the better day is a gold chip under the plate and the rest waits in today's card under More. The caption over the view (why a key moment happens, what looking up shows) docks on the compass plate from 768 px, which names its dots (Sun, Earth, lander); the time bar's flags are laid out by their measured widths in up to three rows.
- `/compare`: "Compare sites". Up to four sites in one table for the same period and lander: a small Sun and Earth strip per site (`src/components/compare/MiniStrip.tsx`), sunlight, Earth in view, longest night, time with power from the period's first day and the verdict, with one sentence above the table that names the best site for each measure, every tied site included (`src/lib/viz/compare.ts`). Empty, it offers two ready pairs; with two sites or more, a link searches the next 12 months for the landing day that is best on average.
- `/validate`: "Accuracy". It opens with "All three checks pass." Three checks, each with its pass mark, headline figure and plain sentence (`src/lib/viz/checks.ts`), and its table behind "Show the numbers": directions to the Sun and Earth against JPL Horizons (the live re-check runs through `/api/horizons`), sunlight against the LOLA illumination map, predicted sunsets against the reported ends of three missions. Then the error budget and the test count; `npm run validation:tests` refreshes the count in `public/validation/tests.json`.
- `/brief`: a printable page for the selected site and period: Explore's answer and its sentences, five numbers (sunlight, contact with Earth, longest night, time with power, battery when full), the Sun's and Earth's rows over the period, the lander, the method and the data, and a paragraph written from the numbers (by a model when one answers and every number checks out, otherwise by a template). The rows keep their black background in print.
- `/learn`: "How it works". The home page's three drawings, the science behind them (the Moon's tilt, libration, how to read the charts, the battery sum) and five steps through the pages (`src/lib/learn/story.ts`), then the way to the site list. It also carries a 150 % projector tip and "Running it offline" (`#offline`).

Every panel ends with a row naming its NASA data (`src/components/Sources.tsx`): LOLA 20 m south of 87.55° S and 80 m elsewhere, JPL DE440, "checked: JPL Horizons" as a link to Accuracy, the NASA CGI Moon Kit for the 3D globe, and on the site list the Artemis III regions and the CLPS mission records.

The 404 and error pages and the plain words for a data failure (`DataProblem`) say what happened and where to go next. The app has an icon and a share image, a title for each page, and `NEXT_PUBLIC_SITE_URL` (see `.env.example`) sets the address the share image links to.
- `/api/chat`: the assistant (task `plan`) and the brief narrator (task `narrate`) on OpenRouter; see `.env.example`. Without a key, or with `?demo=1`, answers come from `src/lib/ai/transcripts.json` and the phrase matcher.

Time with power (`src/lib/engine/landing.ts`) is the hours from landing, with the battery as charged as the lander settings say (full by default), to the first hour the battery cannot cover, counted up to the length of the period. Its Python reference is `pipeline/lunar_pipeline/landing.py`; `uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli fixture-landing --site ID [--start UTC] [--days N]` (from `pipeline/`) writes a golden file into `data/fixtures/landing/` that the TypeScript tests check against.

Terrain and shadows. `uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli terrain` (from `pipeline/`) cuts a height window for every polar site in the catalogue (20 m per pixel inside 87.55° S, 80 m elsewhere), an 80 m context grid around each 20 m window and the 960 m south cap, listed in `data_out/terrain/manifest.json` (version 3: each 20 m window names its 80 m context, and the arrival's site its 240 m flight level, `<site>.flight.u16`, which the R2 mirror must receive before the deployed arrival can draw it; without it the arrival draws as before); `sync-local` copies them to `public/data/terrain` (git-ignored; tests that need them skip without them). The shadow map is one sweep per grid along the Sun's direction, keeping the running maximum of the tangent height minus distance times the tangent of the Sun's elevation, coarse to fine (`src/lib/engine/shadow.ts`): a ±230 km crop of the cap, the context, then the window, so hills outside the window still shade it. It runs in `src/workers/shadow.worker.ts` through `src/lib/shadow-client.ts` (an overtaken hour's picture is dropped): half resolution at once and full resolution once the hour rests (`useShadowPicture`), measured in the browser at about 37 ms and 135 ms for a 1024 by 1024 window. The 3D terrain is painted with the same picture instead of GPU shadows. Its Python reference is `pipeline/lunar_pipeline/shadow.py`; `uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli fixture-shadow` writes `data/fixtures/shadow/`, and `shadow-acceptance.test.ts` checks that de Gerlache Rim 2's pixel is lit when the engine has the Sun up in at least 97 % of the hours of January 2027. `next.config.ts` makes the browser check `/data` files again on every load, since the pipeline rewrites them under the same names.

## The sky (`src/lib/sky/`)

The stand view's maths, each piece tested against SPICE or the engine:

- `frames.ts`: the site's east, north and up in the Moon-fixed frame; azimuth and elevation to world directions (x east, y up, z south).
- `moonOrientation.ts`: the Moon's orientation every 6 hours (`data/sky/moon_me_6h.f32`, J2000 to MOON_ME from SPICE, 2024 to 2036), slerped; 60 off-grid times within 0.001° of SPICE.
- `stars.ts`: the Yale Bright Star Catalogue (`data/sky/stars.bin`, 8,404 stars to magnitude 6.5) placed for a site and hour; within 0.01° of SPICE at two sites and five hours.
- `earth.ts`: Earth's lit fraction and its turn, by the IAU model SPICE uses for IAU_EARTH (within 0.05°).
- `terrainField.ts` and `shadowRule.ts`: the shadow levels as heights in the site's frame (curvature dropped), and the per-pixel shadow rule the ground shader runs: the steepest rise towards the Sun against the Sun's elevation, the Sun's disc for the penumbra, samples from beyond the point's own cell out to 150 km. Its TypeScript twin agrees with the engine on 712 of the 720 hours of January 2027 at de Gerlache Rim 2 (the misses: the Sun half hidden at sunset and sunrise).
- `shaders.ts` and `standRenderer.ts`: the scene (ground meshes from 20 m to 960 m cells out to 125 km, the far skyline as a ring, the stars, the Sun at its true 0.53° with a faint glare, Earth) with regolith light (Lommel–Seeliger, a lunar phase curve, the opposition surge), metre-scale craters and stones drawn near the eye that cast their own shadows, and your own shadow (a person 1.8 m tall). The exposure stops down as a camera's would, looking down-Sun at sunlit ground or with the Sun in the frame; by day the stars show only once the view looks up. The guides: the period's Sun and Earth paths as thin bands, solid where they clear the skyline and faint where the hills hide them, the level horizon all round, and a ring where a hidden Sun or Earth sits (`setGuides`). For the home page's arrival the scene flies: `setPose` puts the camera anywhere with the sky round it, the measured ground draws out to 200 km with the globe (`globe.ts`) beyond it, above 450 km the globe alone shows the Moon, the far skyline ring shows only within 10 m of the ground, and `setScene` swaps in a scene built beside the one on the screen (the ground arriving) once its shaders have compiled. `buildScene` needs no WebGL, so the camera and the labels are tested in Node. Part D2 (immersive design, section 17): the measured ground's meshes live in `groundLevels.ts`, each reading its own three height slots (its level, the next, the cap last) and cut round, its light fading into the next level over the ring inside its edge, and its shadows too within 5 km of the ground, so no square shows from the air; in flight a fourth level, NASA's 240 m heights round the site, draws between the context and the cap with its light from per-pixel slopes. The lander (`landerMesh.ts`, its shadow shape in `landerShape.ts`) has braced legs on dish feet, tanks, thruster pods, payloads and creased gold and silver foil whose glints are capped under the bloom, and its shaded sides are filled by the sunlit dust by day. The rocks (`boulders.ts`) are stones cut by planes in random directions, rounded and bedded, in three kinds. At night with neither the Sun nor Earth up the ground is lit by the sky it sees (slopes by how much they face up, crater bowls darker than their rims) and the rocks and the lander from above, with a faint key light from 50° up and 90° round from the Sun so the stones keep their facets (`NIGHT` in `shaders.ts`). Part D3 (section 18): the opening turns up to 30° towards the skyline's highest point with the Sun and the lander kept in frame (`standOpening.ts`); in flight the 240 m level draws at 960 m cells and, above 5 km, the moving frames go without MSAA with the fast setting's march and a pixel ratio paced by the frames' times (`RatioPacer` in `framePacer.ts`: lowered after three slow frames in a row, raised again after thirty fast ones), so the arrival's 95th-percentile frame on an Intel UHD 620 is 17 ms (D2: 200 ms); a view at rest keeps MSAA; a lander changed in today's card is swapped into the renderer the view has (`setScene`) rather than drawn by a new one; the shaders are compiled with the composer's target bound, which took 15-28 s off a first visit; `setBright` triples the exposure (`BRIGHT_GAIN`) for projectors.
- `landerShape.ts`: the drawn lander's shape in one table; the model is built from it and the ground shader casts its soft shadow from it (the Sun's disc as the penumbra), so the two always match.
- `boulders.ts`: fourteen drawn boulders and 360 pebbles, seeded by the site and kept clear of the lander and the line to it; the ground shader casts the boulders' shadows.
- `framePacer.ts`: when the view draws: every frame while something moves, one sharp frame after 300 ms of rest, then nothing; a slow graphics chip never queues frames it has not finished. It also says when the view has come to rest (`atRest`), even when the moving frames were already at full sharpness, which the arrival's capture hook waits for.

`uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli fetch --group sky`, then `sky` and `fixture-sky` (from `pipeline/`), write `data/sky/` and `data/fixtures/sky/`; `sync-local` copies `data/sky` to `public/data/sky`.

## The arrival (`src/components/home/`)

The home page opens with the arrival: a section seven screens tall whose stage stays in the window while the page scrolls, from the whole Moon down to landing at de Gerlache Rim 2 at 10:00 UTC on 1 January 2027, then that month at the site. The first screen is the whole Moon with Earth just beyond its south pole, and the headline; then one screen a beat: straight down on the pole from 900 km, the polar cap from 90 km, the ridge from 2 km (looking past the site at the Sun just over the hills), landing beside the lander, the month at the site half run (the hours go on, sped up, once and in order), and its end with the answer for the site. "Fly to the south pole" plays it in 21 seconds, 15 to landing and 6 through the month (a wheel, a touch or a key stops it; at the end the answer takes the focus), and Skip, shown part-way, lands on the answer. With reduced motion the stage holds each beat's view, with no flight between them and no hours running, and "Fly to the south pole" lands at once. The words are page text in reading order; without JavaScript "Fly to the south pole" is a link to `/sites`. The answer's actions say where they go: "Open the full forecast" (Explore for the site) and "Choose a landing site" (the list).

- `Arrival.tsx`: the section: the words of each screen, the scroll as the flight's progress (0 to 5), Play, Skip, reduced motion, the stills and the capture address. A beat's words fade as they scroll up to the top, so they never pass over the stage's date bar there. The stage comes through `next/dynamic` without server rendering, so three.js loads after the page's words.
- `ArrivalCanvas.tsx`: the live stage. It flies over the sky and the globe as soon as they load and swaps in the site's measured ground when it comes (the 20 m window, the context grid, the 240 m flight level round the site when the data has it, and the south cap out to 460 km; without the ground the flight goes on over the globe, and without the flight file it draws as before). Labels name Earth and the pole from space, the site on the way down (from the cap down before the pole's, which gives way), and once landed the Sun and Earth where they show; out of view, a pointer at the view's edge, which once landed stands on the left or right edge at the body's height, its arrow along the horizon. A label never sits under a beat's card: the night's name and the pointers move above or below a card that would cover them, the pole's name takes another side of its point, and the other labels give way while a card covers them; every label also waits where it would fall on words with no plate (the first screen's headline) or on a label already placed. Once landed a date bar at the top, placed first, gives the date with a moment's name for a day after it passes, else what lights the ground ("in sunlight", "night, in blue Earthlight (picture brightened)", "night (picture brightened)"), and the hour's mark on the month, the four moments ticked in their bodies' colours with a key. The month's Sun and Earth paths show, dotted where the hills hide them, each named over its line while it is the story (the Sun's until Earth comes into view, Earth's until the Sun comes back; on a phone, none), and the level horizon is left out. `?quality=fast` lightens it as in the stand view. When the sky or the Moon does not load or the graphics fail, it gives way to the stills.
- `src/lib/sky/arrivalTiming.ts`: the flight's stops (`STOPS`: 0, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 5), where each beat's words stand (`BEAT_STOPS`: 0, 1.25, 2, 3, 4, 4.5, 5), the month at the site (`SITE`, progress 4 to 5: from the landing hour to five days after the Sun comes back, at an even pace; `hourAt`), the scroll as progress and back, and Play's seconds from stop to stop (`PLAY`, 21 in all). It has no three.js, so the section can import it.
- `src/lib/sky/arrivalPath.ts`: the flight's keys from the real Sun and Earth directions at the hour (`flightKeys`: the whole Moon from 9,000 km, the pole from 900 km, the cap from 90 km up and 150 km back with the Sun at its side, the ridge from 2 km up and 5 km back, then landing beside the lander) and the camera at any progress (`poseAt`: the height on a log scale, never under the ground). On a wide screen what each beat is about stands right of the middle, clear of the words. Beside the lander (`BESIDE`) the camera stands a few metres up behind it, on a wide screen the lander right of the words' column (the beats' cards are at most 46 per cent of the width) and the Sun or Earth further right, on a phone the lander under the Sun or Earth; through the month at the site (`siteTrack`, `aimAt`) it stands back from the lander along each aim, so it circles the lander as the story turns from the Sun going behind the hills to Earth coming into view and the Sun coming back.
- `src/lib/sky/globe.ts`: the Moon as a globe: NASA's colour map and relief on a sphere of 512 by 256 cells placed in the site's frame, lit as the ground is (Lommel–Seeliger, the same phase curve and opposition surge), with its own long shadows by the engine's rule over its heights (28 steps out to about 220 km). It leaves out the 200 km round the site where the measured ground draws.
- `src/lib/data/globe.ts`: loads `globe/manifest.json` and the globe's heights from the data base; the colour and normal maps come at 1K first and at 4K when they arrive.
- `src/lib/viz/arrival.ts`: the site and month for the stage (`hero()`, from `home.json`, with the engine's power) and the arrival's words, worked out on the server (`arrivalWords()`): the five beat sentences, the standing line, Explore's answer as the stand view words it ("Landing on 1 January: power for 3 days 4 hours, no radio contact, then 16 days without power"), the better landing day and Explore's address.
- `src/lib/viz/standOpening.ts`: where the lander stands (16 m away, placed by the hour the stand view opens on, so its first view holds it; the arrival places it by the period's first hour and lands beside it there) and where the stand view's first view looks.
- `public/arrival/beat-<k>-<wide|tall>.jpg`: a still for each screen (k from 0 to 6, at the progress of `BEAT_STOPS`), wide, and tall for portrait screens. They are the first paint, stand in until the stage has drawn, and stay when it cannot draw (no WebGL 2, a failed shader, the sky or the Moon not loading).

Capture. `/?arrival=<p>`, with `p` from 0 to 5, holds the flight at that progress and ignores the scroll; `&bare=1` leaves the words out, so only the stage and its labels show. On such an address the stage, once it has loaded, sets `window.__arrival = { setProgress(p), sharp() }`: `setProgress` moves the flight, and `sharp()` returns a promise that resolves once the frame at that progress is on the screen at full sharpness (at the next frame if the view already rests there). To remake the stills, capture each screen's stop from the production build in a 1440 by 900 and a 390 by 844 window, with the arrival scrolled to the top of the window and the shot clipped to the stage. The video's opening frames come the same way (`docs/video-storyboard.md`).

The globe's files come from the pipeline (from `pipeline/`): `uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli fetch --group globe`, then `globe`; `sync-local --tiles` copies them to `public/data/globe` (about 5.3 MB, git-ignored). The site's month for the arrival is in `src/lib/data/home.json` (see "Home page assets").

## Themes

Dark is the default (part C of the immersive design); the header switch changes it and the choice is kept in `localStorage` under `earthrise.theme`, so a visitor who chose light keeps it. The light theme stays for print and classrooms, and the printable brief is always light. `?theme=dark` or `?theme=light` sets the theme for one visit without changing the stored choice. The system preference is not followed. An inline script in `src/app/layout.tsx` sets `data-theme` on `<html>` before the first paint (`src/lib/theme.ts`). Colours are tokens in `src/app/globals.css` (the dark values also apply inside `.theme-dark`, which the stand view uses so its time bar stays dark under the black sky); picture panels ("stages": charts on black, the shadow map, 3D) keep the same dark colours in both themes. The design is in `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-01-ui-redesign-design.md`.

Text never scales with a picture: labels on charts and drawings are page text laid over the drawing (`src/components/SkyRow.tsx`), so nothing falls under 13 px on a narrow screen.

The look, in short (reasoning in section 15 of the redesign, and sections 6 and 15 of the immersive design): the palette is the Moon's own three tones, shadow black for picture panels, regolith grey for the light page and sunlit white for its panels, with colour kept for the Sun (gold) and Earth (blue). In the dark theme the page is near-black (`#07080a`), the stages true black, and panels are dark plates one step above the page with a hairline edge; body text is 7:1 on them and secondary text 4.5:1 in both themes (`src/app/globals.test.ts`). Nothing is blurred and nothing has a gradient or a glow (`src/app/look.test.ts`). Faint real stars from the stand view's catalogue run behind the header and each page's title (`StarBand`, `src/lib/sky/starBand.ts`): the low sky over the south pole, drawn on a canvas down to the element marked `data-band-end`, in the dark theme only. Headlines use the class `display` (Bricolage Grotesque at 87.5 % width, weight 700). Pictures have a 6 px radius, panels and controls 8 px. Nothing moves by itself but the arrival, which moves only as the page scrolls or after "Fly to the south pole" is pressed; reduced motion shows still frames.

## Assistant and narration

The assistant is the panel "Ask in plain words" on Explore and Compare (`src/components/PlannerChat.tsx`). `src/lib/ai/`: `actions.ts` (closed action schema, validator, site matcher, `applyActions`, the notes in the pages' words), `intents.ts` (twelve rehearsed phrasings, among them "make it 90 days" and "find the best landing date for 14 days"), `explanations.ts` (five fixed paragraphs), `state-summary.ts` (the compact state sent with each question), `openrouter.ts` (model chain with timeouts), `protocol.ts` (system prompt, lenient JSON extraction, reply types), `cache.ts` + `transcripts.json` (twelve rehearsed answers), `rate-limit.ts`, `narrate.ts` (number check and template brief), `client.ts` (browser calls). The number check removes any number in a reply that the app's figures do not hold. The example questions under the box ask at once when clicked and come back when nothing answered. While a question is out, a wait row shows it, the seconds, and after 8 s "Free models can be slow; if none answers, the app replies from its own phrasings." When a key is set and every model fails, the reply is the busy one (`reason: "model-busy"`). "Or ask in plain words" on the answer jumps to the box (`#ask`). `AI_MODEL_TIMEOUT_MS` sets the time one model gets and `AI_MODEL_BUDGET_MS` the total per request (see `.env.example`). The catalogue the server validates against is `src/lib/data/catalogue.json`, written by the pipeline's `sync-local`.

## Power and contact

- `src/lib/engine/power.ts`: irradiance, panel geometries, battery simulation (`powerBudget`), pinned to `data/fixtures/power/` written by `pipeline/lunar_pipeline/power.py`.
- `src/lib/engine/metrics.ts`: `withCommsThreshold`, `contactWindows`, `windowScore`, and `Series.sunAu`.
