# Method (Week 1 reference implementation)

## Sun and Earth positions
- SPICE DE440 (`de440s.bsp`), lunar orientation `moon_pa_de440_200625.bpc`, frame `MOON_ME` (alias of `MOON_ME_DE440_ME421`), aberration `LT+S`, observer = Moon centre.
- Hourly table 2024-01-01T00:00 → 2036-12-31T23:00 (113,976 steps), float32 km, Catmull-Rom interpolation between samples (error below 0.002° at half steps).
- Topocentric az/el from a site on the 1737.4 km sphere with the site offset subtracted (Earth parallax up to 0.26°). Sun/Earth directions are evaluated with site height 0 m (matches the Horizons fixtures at alt = 0); the DEM height + mast enter the horizon mask only. Effect on az/el is below 0.001°.

## Terrain horizon
- LOLA polar DEMs (20 m to 6 km, 80 m to 100 km, 240 m to 220 km), 720 azimuths, great-circle march, exact spherical curvature, bilinear sampling. Observer mast 2 m.
- Where a ring leaves its DEM (sites near a grid edge, e.g. IM-1 at 80.13°S), the missing distances are re-marched on the next coarser DEM instead of being dropped.
- Sunlit fraction = fraction of the solar disk (radius 0.267°) above the skyline; the Sun counts as up when any part of the disk is above it. Earth visible when the Earth centre is above the skyline (the LOLA Earth map counts any part of Earth's disc, about 0.95° in radius, which gives more hours; see Validation).

## Cross-checks (from `data/fixtures/horizons/summary.json` and the report command)
- JPL Horizons, 6 sites × 2 bodies × 25 epochs (300 rows, 2025–2026): max |Δel| = 2.7e-5°, max |Δaz·cos el| = 2.6e-6° (about 0.1 arcsec).
- Mazarico et al. 2011 maps at the Shackleton rim high point (−89.781°, −156.257°). Two of our quantities are comparable: `illum_fraction` = fraction of hours with any part of the solar disk above the skyline (0.907 over 2025–2026), and `mean_sun_fraction` = time-averaged visible disk fraction (0.862). The map value `AVGVISIB` = 0.796 (hourly over 18.6 years, 60 m DEM, from the ground; the label's 2009–2013 START_TIME/STOP_TIME is the span of the LOLA data, not the averaging period). Earth visibility: ours 0.500 vs map 0.533. Systematic differences: our point is the 20 m argmax peak (map pixel is 60 m), we add a 2 m mast, and the spans differ; the sign (ours higher) is consistent with all three.
- Shackleton crater centre: 0 % sunlit and 0 % Earth-visible in both our series and the Mazarico maps (permanently shadowed).
- Longest continuous darkness at the Shackleton rim high point over 2025–2026: 63 h; longest Earth gap: 334 h.

## Power budget (Week 4)

Irradiance on a Sun-facing surface is 1361 W/m² · (1 AU / d)² · f, where d is the Sun distance from the DE440 table and f the visible fraction of the solar disk above the terrain skyline (section "Terrain horizon"). Panel output is P = η · A · irradiance · max(0, n · s) with the panel normal n for four geometries: horizontal (n = up, n · s = sin el), vertical fixed at azimuth φ (cos el · cos(az − φ)), vertical turning to the Sun's azimuth (cos el) and two-axis tracking (1). The battery walk uses one-hour steps: E(t+1) = min(C, E(t) + (P − L) · 1 h); an hour whose deficit exceeds the stored energy is a brownout and leaves the battery empty. Reported: minimum state of charge, brownout hours, first brownout hour, and the time with power (for example "power for 3 days 4 hours, then night"). Reference implementation: `pipeline/lunar_pipeline/power.py`; golden fixtures under `data/fixtures/power/` pin `web/src/lib/engine/power.ts` to 1e-6 W. The default configuration (vertical panel turning to the Sun, 4 m², a 30 % panel, 200 W load, 2000 Wh battery) is illustrative of a small lander, not any specific vehicle.

## Direct-to-Earth contact (Week 4)

Earth is in view when its elevation exceeds the skyline at its azimuth by the minimum elevation the planner sets (0–10°). Contact windows are maximal runs of visible hours. Mast height changes the skyline itself: the browser worker recomputes the selected site's skyline with the new mast; featured skylines are computed with a 2 m mast.

## Best window (Week 4)

The finder scans the 365 days after the chosen start date (clamped to the ephemeris table) in 6-hour steps and scores every window of the chosen length as sunlit fraction + Earth-visible fraction − (longest night + longest Earth gap) / (2 × window hours). Only sunlit start hours are candidates, because a lander arrives in daylight; a site that never sees the Sun in the scan falls back to every start. Without this rule a window that begins mid-night wins by truncating the night at its edge.

## Validation

`cd pipeline && uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli validate` writes four tables into `data/validation/` (copied into the app by `sync-local` and shown on `/validate`). It downloads any input that is missing (the SPICE kernels, the three LOLA grids, the illumination maps and `ldem_85s_20m`, about 1 GB on a fresh checkout) and takes about five minutes. Each table opens with an `evidence` block: what it establishes, its criteria (none for context), its coverage, its limitations and the command. There are four kinds of evidence, and only the first three are checks.

1. **Directions** (`horizons.json`, a check). Establishes that our hourly DE440 table (MOON_ME, LT+S) and our topocentric azimuth and elevation reproduce JPL Horizons' apparent, airless azimuth and elevation for the same coordinates (`CENTER=coord@301`, geodetic at 0 km, `QUANTITIES=4`, `APPARENT=AIRLESS`). Criteria: every row within 0.01° in elevation and in azimuth × cos(elevation). Coverage: six sites (south pole, Shackleton and de Gerlache crater centres, IM-1, IM-2, Blue Ghost Mission 1) × Sun and Earth × 25 epochs, 2025-01-01 to 2026-12-22 every 30 days at 00:00 UTC: 300 rows, worst 2.7e-5° in elevation and 2.6e-6° in azimuth × cos(el). The page can re-query JPL for eight epochs in 2026 through `/api/horizons` (a server-side proxy with a 12 s timeout) and falls back to the stored table when JPL is unreachable. Limits: geometry only, no terrain; both sides rest on JPL's DE4xx ephemerides and lunar orientation, so this checks how we use them, not the ephemerides; every stored epoch falls on the table's hour grid, so interpolation between hours is checked separately (the pipeline's tests, against SPICE at half steps, under 0.0001°); observers at 0 m on the 1737.4 km sphere; 2025 to 2026 only.

2. **Terrain illumination** (`mazarico.json`, a check). The reference is the LOLA average-visibility pair `AVGVISIB_85S_060M_201608` and `AVGVISIB_85S_060M_201608_EARTH` (method of Mazarico et al. 2011, Icarus 211, 1066-1081; 2016 release; labels as revised on 2023-10-13 at the LOLA node and PDS Geosciences). What the products are, from their own metadata:
   - Quantity: "the percentage of timesteps where each pixel was sunlit (by any fraction of the solar disc)" (the label's DESCRIPTION); for Earth, any part of Earth's disc (PGDA product 69). The label's image object calls the value "average solar disc visibility", which could also be read as the mean visible fraction of the disc; the comparison settles it: worked out the map's way, the mean visible fraction runs a median 3.0 points below the map, the share of hours with any part of the disc 0.9 points from it.
   - Period and sampling: hourly time steps spanning 18.6 years, one lunar precession cycle (PGDA product 69; the 2014 release notes say the same of the earlier 240 m maps); the dates are not stated. The label's START_TIME and STOP_TIME (2009-07-13 to 2013-07-18) are the span of the LOLA altimetry, not the averaging period. The 2011 paper's abstract also describes runs over several 18.6-year cycles at 6 h steps; the product page says the maps average hourly steps.
   - Observer: on the lunar surface (the 2014 release notes, of the earlier 240 m maps); the product page leaves height above the surface to further analysis.
   - Terrain and grid: LDEM_85S_060M, 60 m per pixel, polar stereographic on the 1737.4 km sphere, true scale at the pole, 5058 × 5058 pixels, pixel-registered.
   - Frame: MEAN EARTH/POLAR AXIS OF DE421 (label). Our MOON_ME is MOON_ME_DE440_ME421, which NAIF's frame kernel aligns with it to 5.8e-7 rad, about 1 m on the ground, over 1900 to 2051; the map's ephemeris is not stated (ours is DE440).
   - Method: horizon elevations in 720 directions (0.5°) and the disc cut by a straight skyline at its centre's direction, with limb darkening (as Barker et al. 2021, Planet. Space Sci. 203, 105119, describe it).

   We work each covered site out one change at a time: **app** (2 m mast, LOLA 20/80/240 m, hourly 2024-2036, any part of the disc), **surface** (the same from the ground), **cycle** (and hourly over 18.6 years from 2024-01-01, 6,798 days), **matched** (and on 60 m posts made from 3 × 3 means of LDEM_85S_20M, aligned with the map's pixel centres, then LOLA 80 m where they end and 240 m from 100 to 220 km, worked out at the map's four pixel centres around the site and combined with the map's bilinear weights). The gap is matched minus map, in percentage points. For Earth we give the app's rule (Earth's centre above the skyline) and the map's (any part of a 6,371 km disc).

   Criteria: the median gap over the covered sites is 5 points or less for the Sun map and for the Earth map; a site agrees within 5 points. Results (generated 2026-11-14): 15 of the 18 polar sites are on the map (IM-1, Nobile Rim 2 and the peak near Cabeus B lie outside its square, which reaches 85° S on every side). Sunlight: median 0.9 points, 14 of 15 within 5 and 12 within 2. Earth in view: median 0.2 points, 14 of 15 within 5. The one site beyond 5 points is the pole itself (+7.0 for the Sun, +6.1 for Earth): one of its four map pixels reads 0 % beside neighbours at 27 to 30 % (within about 1 km of the pole the maps have zero pixels among lit ones), and without that pixel the gaps are −0.3 and +1.2 points. The app's own numbers sit a median 2.6 points from the Sun map (worst +10.8 at the Shackleton rim high point) and 4.1 points from the Earth map, because of what the app does differently on purpose: the 2 m mast raises the sunlit share by a median 1.4 points (19 points on the connecting ridge's 20 m terrain), finer terrain resolves narrow peaks and slopes the 60 m map smooths, and the Earth-centre rule counts fewer hours than any part of the disc. Using 2024-2036 (13 years) instead of the full cycle moves the shares by at most 0.3 points. The assumptions we could not match are listed in `evidence.unmatched`: the averaging dates, the map's own DEM (no longer archived), how far its skylines reach beyond its grid, its ephemeris (the frames themselves agree to about 1 m), the march's steps, limb darkening (it matters only for the mean disc fraction), Earth's radius, and the label's own slips (the Earth label gives MINIMUM_LATITUDE 90 and says "sunlit"; the 2016 release label gave CENTER_LONGITUDE 180 and INT32).

   Limits: both sides are models built from LOLA altimetry with similar methods, so errors they share cancel; this is agreement between two models, not a ground observation (Mazarico et al. 2011 compared their model with LROC WAC images). Averages over 18.6 years can hide errors in the hour of single sunrises and sunsets.

3. **Power sums** (`power.json`, a check). Establishes that the panel and battery arithmetic gives the answers worked out by hand formula, in the pipeline's model (`power.py`) and in the app's engine (`power.ts`, run in the browser on the Accuracy page). Criteria: every worked case within 1e-6 W and 1e-6 Wh, with brownout hours and the first brownout hour exactly equal. Coverage: nine worked cases (37 hours, all four panel models), among them full sunshine at 1 AU (0.3 × 4 m² × 1361 W/m² = 1633.2 W), half the disc, 0.983 AU, a vertical panel 60° off the Sun, the Sun behind a vertical panel, a flat panel at 3°, the Sun 1° below the flat horizon, a night on a full battery and a recharge from half full; and the five recorded runs in `data/fixtures/power/` (two sites, 6,480 hours), which the pipeline reruns against its record and `web/src/lib/engine/power.test.ts` compares with the app's engine. Limits: it checks the sums, not hardware (no panel heating, dust, ageing, wiring or battery losses, heaters or real loads); the worked answers use the same formulas, so a wrong formula would pass.

4. **Mission context** (`backcasts.json`, not a check: no criteria and no pass mark). Our predicted local sunset after each CLPS landing (LOLA skyline, or a flat horizon for Blue Ghost's mare site) beside the end its operator reported: Blue Ghost Mission 1 ended 2.4 h after our predicted sunset (Firefly reported about 5 h after the sunset it saw, so our flat-horizon sunset is about 2.5 h late there); IM-1 ended 67 h before it, tipped over with its panels away from the low Sun; IM-2 299 h before it, on its side in a crater. It can tell whether our sunset comes before or after a reported end when a lander ran into the night, and that the Sun was still up when IM-1 and IM-2 ended. It cannot tell the hour of sunset (mission ends are reported events, not observed sunsets, and two have no published hour: 00:00 and 12:00 UTC are used), whether a battery lasts the night, or how often the forecast is right (one case is not a sample).

Error budget: Sun and Earth directions under 0.01° (measured 3e-5°); terrain skyline 0.05-0.15° (LOLA posts of 20-240 m, 1-2 m height noise, reproduced by the browser engine to 0.01°); event timing ±15-60 minutes near the pole (hourly series, the Sun moves 0.5° per hour along the horizon).
