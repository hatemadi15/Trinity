# Method (Week 1 reference implementation)

## Sun and Earth positions
- SPICE DE440 (`de440s.bsp`), lunar orientation `moon_pa_de440_200625.bpc`, frame `MOON_ME` (alias of `MOON_ME_DE440_ME421`), aberration `LT+S`, observer = Moon centre.
- Hourly table 2024-01-01T00:00 → 2036-12-31T23:00 (113,976 steps), float32 km, Catmull-Rom interpolation between samples (error below 0.002° at half steps).
- Topocentric az/el from a site on the 1737.4 km sphere with the site offset subtracted (Earth parallax up to 0.26°). Sun/Earth directions are evaluated with site height 0 m (matches the Horizons fixtures at alt = 0); the DEM height + mast enter the horizon mask only. Effect on az/el is below 0.001°.

## Terrain horizon
- LOLA polar DEMs (20 m to 6 km, 80 m to 100 km, 240 m to 220 km), 720 azimuths, great-circle march, exact spherical curvature, bilinear sampling. Observer mast 2 m for the featured skylines; 0 to 10 m in the browser.
- Where a ring leaves its DEM (sites near a grid edge, e.g. IM-1 at 80.13°S), the missing distances are re-marched on the next coarser DEM instead of being dropped.
- North of 80°S only the 240 m DEM reaches a site (Chandrayaan-3 at 69.37°S, the Luna 25 target, Schrödinger basin at 74.73°S): the 80 m band finds no data and the whole skyline, from 40 m to 220 km, is marched on the 240 m grid, as the browser engine does (the parity test reproduces these masks to 0.01°). Their 3D terrain is a 240 m window, 61 km across. Sites north of 60°S lie outside the polar DEMs and keep a flat horizon, as Blue Ghost Mission 1 does.
- Sunlit fraction = fraction of the solar disk (radius 0.267°) above the skyline; the Sun counts as up when any part of the disk is above it. Earth visible when the Earth centre is above the skyline (the LOLA Earth map counts any part of Earth's disc, about 0.95° in radius, which gives more hours; see Validation).

## Terrain completeness and mast height

A skyline is kept, and worked out, by everything that changes it: the site's position, the mast height and the terrain source. A featured skyline (`data/masks`, made by the pipeline at 2 m) stands for 2 m only. At any other height the browser worker works out the skyline of every site a shown result uses: the selected site, the compared sites, the site list (and with it Explore's nearby search) and the year search. Going from 2 m to 5 m lowers the featured skylines by 0.01° to 0.64° on average and by up to 2.2° in one direction (the peak near Cabeus B), so a 2 m skyline is not a 5 m one. The worker runs one wanted job at a time (the selected site first, then the compared sites, then the list); a job for a height or a site nobody shows any more is dropped before its turn, and one already running is let finish without holding up the rest. Every change of settings commits the settings, the results and the sites still waiting in one update, so no state shows a result from older settings; while a site waits, the page says so ("Working out the hills seen from a 5 m mast for 18 sites… The figures below are still from 2 m.") and every result carries the height its skyline is seen from. A skyline that cannot be had says why: the tiles are not on this server, a tile failed to load, or the point lies north of 60° S.

Every skyline also carries a terrain report to every result (the verdict, each compared site, each row of the site list, the brief):

- Provenance: a featured file (its name, when the pipeline made it, at its mast height), a skyline worked out in the browser from the tiles (the mast height, when the tile set was cut), or a flat horizon assumed for a site outside the polar grids (the same from any mast).
- Coverage: each of the 720 rays is counted in 120 m steps out to 220 km (the 240 m grid's half pixel); a step has terrain when any band, primary or fallback, gave a height in it. The report gives the share of steps with terrain, the missing share within 6 km, from 6 to 100 km and from 100 to 220 km, and the nearest missing step.
- Open rays: a missing step counts as no hill, so it can only lower the skyline. It matters only where a hill there could stand above the skyline found; the bound is the highest ground on the Moon (10,786 m above the 1737.4 km sphere) placed at that step. A ray with such a step is open: its skyline is not established.
- Fallback: the bands that took distances from a coarser grid, and the grid the site's own height came from.
- Tiles: per skyline, the tiles the march needed that the server does not have (HTTP 404, a missing file: absent) and those whose load failed (any other error: failed). Every tile of the three grids exists in the manifest, so an absent tile is always a loss.

Each result is then complete (every step has terrain from the grid planned for its distance, no tile absent or failed), degraded (the skyline is established everywhere, but a coarser grid stood in for part of a ray or for the site height, a tile was filled from a coarser grid, steps are missing only where no hill could rise above the skyline, or a flat horizon is assumed) or incomplete (at least one open ray, or a computed skyline without the march's record). The threshold for incomplete is one open ray rather than a share: a missing tile is 41 km (80 m grid) to 123 km (240 m grid) wide and opens rays by the dozen, never one, and one hill in the Sun's path shortens the sunlight by hours. Because missing terrain can only overstate the hours of sunlight and of Earth in view, an incomplete result names no better landing day, no better place nearby, no best site for a measure on Compare (a site with complete terrain that reaches the same top is still named), no longest time with power on the site list and no best date in the next 12 months, and says why; on the site list it is ranked after the others in its group. Degraded results and flat horizons are shown with their limit in words and may still be recommended.

Measured: worked out again from the tiles, all 18 featured skylines have terrain at every one of their 1,319,760 steps and no open ray; only IM-1's takes part of its skyline from the 240 m grid, from 3.9 to 100 km (the 80 m grid ends 4 km north of it). Before, the march counted every sample of a band that ran off its grid as missing even where a fallback band covered it, and called IM-1 32 % missing (1,573,616 of 4,895,280 samples). Dropping one tile under the Shackleton rim summit: the 80 m tile is filled from the 240 m grid from 6 km out (nothing missing, degraded); the 240 m tile leaves 95 steps without terrain, all 100 to 220 km out, and opens 7 rays (incomplete). Under de Gerlache Rim 2 a lost 240 m tile leaves 7,598 missing steps but opens no ray (degraded); under Malapert Massif it leaves 19 and opens 3 (incomplete). A skyline takes 0.2 to 1.0 s to work out in Node from local tiles, so a new mast height on the site list (18 polar sites) takes some seconds in a browser.

## Cross-checks (from `data/fixtures/horizons/summary.json` and the report command)
- JPL Horizons, 6 sites × 2 bodies × 25 epochs (300 rows, 2025–2026): max |Δel| = 2.7e-5°, max |Δaz·cos el| = 2.6e-6° (about 0.1 arcsec).
- Mazarico et al. 2011 maps at the Shackleton rim high point (−89.781°, −156.257°). Two of our quantities are comparable: `illum_fraction` = fraction of hours with any part of the solar disk above the skyline (0.907 over 2025–2026), and `mean_sun_fraction` = time-averaged visible disk fraction (0.862). The map value `AVGVISIB` = 0.796 (hourly over 18.6 years, 60 m DEM, from the ground; the label's 2009–2013 START_TIME/STOP_TIME is the span of the LOLA data, not the averaging period). Earth visibility: ours 0.500 vs map 0.533. Systematic differences: our point is the 20 m argmax peak (map pixel is 60 m), we add a 2 m mast, and the spans differ; the sign (ours higher) is consistent with all three.
- Shackleton crater centre: 0 % sunlit and 0 % Earth-visible in both our series and the Mazarico maps (permanently shadowed).
- Longest continuous darkness at the Shackleton rim high point over 2025–2026: 63 h; longest Earth gap: 334 h.

## Power budget (Week 4)

Irradiance on a Sun-facing surface is 1361 W/m² · (1 AU / d)² · f, where d is the Sun distance from the DE440 table and f the visible fraction of the solar disk above the terrain skyline (section "Terrain horizon"). Panel output is P = η · A · irradiance · max(0, n · s) with the panel normal n for four geometries: horizontal (n = up, n · s = sin el), vertical fixed at azimuth φ (cos el · cos(az − φ)), vertical turning to the Sun's azimuth (cos el) and two-axis tracking (1). The battery walk uses one-hour steps: E(t+1) = min(C, E(t) + (P − L) · 1 h); an hour whose deficit exceeds the stored energy is a brownout and leaves the battery empty. Reported: minimum state of charge, brownout hours, first brownout hour, and the time with power (for example "power for 3 days 4 hours, then night"). Reference implementation: `pipeline/lunar_pipeline/power.py`; golden fixtures under `data/fixtures/power/` pin `web/src/lib/engine/power.ts` to 1e-6 W. The default configuration (vertical panel turning to the Sun, 4 m², a 30 % panel, 200 W load, 2000 Wh battery) is illustrative of a small lander, not any specific vehicle.

## Direct-to-Earth contact (Week 4)

Earth is in view when its elevation exceeds the skyline at its azimuth by the minimum elevation the planner sets (0–10°). Contact windows are maximal runs of visible hours. Mast height changes the skyline itself: featured skylines are computed with a 2 m mast and stand for that height only; at any other height the browser worker works out the skyline of every site a result shows (see "Terrain completeness and mast height").

This is geometric direct-to-Earth visibility: a clear line from the site to the centre of Earth's disc. It does not by itself establish antenna performance, a link budget, ground-station availability or a scheduled contact, and the app says so once beside its radio windows.

Two conventions, kept apart: the Sun counts as up while any part of its disc is above the skyline (sunlit fraction > 0); Earth counts as in view by its centre, so its disc (about 2° wide from the Moon) is half risen when it starts counting. The status sentence, the numbers, the countdown and the radio windows all read these same two flags.

Radio windows (`web/src/lib/viz/contact.ts`, built on the engine's `contactWindows`): each window's first and last hour on the hourly grid (UTC, the first whole hour of the new state, so the change itself falls within the hour before) and its length; the hours with Earth in view in all; the longest stretch without Earth, counting the stretches cut by the period's start or end, which are reported as "at least" (its length equals the summary's longest Earth gap); and powered contact, the hours with Earth in view and the lander powered, window by window. Powered contact is split at the first power cut: the hours before it are the radio contact while the lander has power that the answer, the numbers card, the brief and Compare give; the hours after the power comes back are reported apart, as they hold only if the lander survives the cold without power, which the energy model does not work out.

Countdown (`web/src/lib/viz/countdown.ts`): at an hour of the period, whether the Sun and Earth are up by the conventions above, and the hours to the first hour with the other state (the hour the key moments give the event). A change that does not come before the period ends is reported as such, never extrapolated. The Sun's elevation is the topocentric elevation above the level horizon (not the skyline), at the time bar's hour and at its highest in the period.

## The period and the best landing (Week 4)

A period is a landing on a whole UTC hour and a length of 2 to 90 whole days. The table's rows fall on whole UTC hours and the engine reads the row nearest the landing, so a landing given with minutes is read at the nearest hour, half an hour and more rounding up; the period boxes, links, the assistant and Luna all do the same (`web/src/lib/period.ts`). The first landing offered is 2 January 2024, 00:00 UTC, and a period must end by 1 January 2037, 00:00 UTC, the hour after the table's last row: a 90-day period can start no later than 3 October 2036, a 7-day one no later than 25 December 2036. Moving the landing by hours or days keeps the length and stops at either end with a plain sentence. The length stops at 90 days because the cost grows with it (the site list works out every site for up to twice the period, the 12-month search a year and a period at each site); it starts at 2 days because every sentence of a forecast counts its days in the plural.

The better landing inside a period is the sunlit hour, tried every hour, that gives the longest time with power, more contact with Earth breaking ties. The 12-month search tries every sixth hour from the landing over the next 365 days (clamped to the table), keeps only landings that leave room for the whole period, scores them the same way, and moves the period to the best one with its hour. Only sunlit landing hours are candidates, because a lander arrives in daylight; a site with no sunlit hour in the scan is reported, not moved.

## Validation

`cd pipeline && uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli validate` writes four tables into `data/validation/` (copied into the app by `sync-local` and shown on `/validate`). It downloads any input that is missing (the SPICE kernels, the three LOLA grids, the illumination maps and `ldem_85s_20m`, about 1 GB on a fresh checkout) and takes about five minutes. Each table opens with an `evidence` block: what it establishes, its criteria (none for context), its coverage, its limitations and the command. There are four kinds of evidence, and only the first three are checks.

1. **Directions** (`horizons.json`, a check). Establishes that our hourly DE440 table (MOON_ME, LT+S) and our topocentric azimuth and elevation reproduce JPL Horizons' apparent, airless azimuth and elevation for the same coordinates (`CENTER=coord@301`, geodetic at 0 km, `QUANTITIES=4`, `APPARENT=AIRLESS`). Criteria: every row within 0.01° in elevation and in azimuth × cos(elevation). Coverage: six sites (south pole, Shackleton and de Gerlache crater centres, IM-1, IM-2, Blue Ghost Mission 1) × Sun and Earth × 25 epochs, 2025-01-01 to 2026-12-22 every 30 days at 00:00 UTC: 300 rows, worst 2.7e-5° in elevation and 2.6e-6° in azimuth × cos(el). The page can re-query JPL for eight epochs in 2026 through `/api/horizons` (a server-side proxy with a 12 s timeout) and falls back to the stored table when JPL is unreachable. Limits: geometry only, no terrain; both sides rest on JPL's DE4xx ephemerides and lunar orientation, so this checks how we use them, not the ephemerides; every stored epoch falls on the table's hour grid, so interpolation between hours is checked separately (the pipeline's tests, against SPICE at half steps, under 0.0001°); observers at 0 m on the 1737.4 km sphere; 2025 to 2026 only.

2. **Terrain illumination** (`mazarico.json`, a check). The reference is the LOLA average-visibility pair `AVGVISIB_85S_060M_201608` and `AVGVISIB_85S_060M_201608_EARTH` (method of Mazarico et al. 2011, Icarus 211, 1066-1081; 2016 release; labels as revised on 2023-10-13 at the LOLA node and PDS Geosciences). What the products are, from their own metadata:
   - Quantity: "the percentage of timesteps where each pixel was sunlit (by any fraction of the solar disc)" (the label's DESCRIPTION); for Earth, any part of Earth's disc (PGDA product 69). The label's image object calls the value "average solar disc visibility", which could also be read as the mean visible fraction of the disc; the comparison settles it: worked out the map's way, the mean visible fraction runs a median 3.0 points below the map, the share of hours with any part of the disc 0.9 points from it.
   - Period and sampling: hourly time steps spanning 18.6 years, one lunar precession cycle (PGDA product 69; the 2014 release notes say the same of the earlier 240 m maps); the dates are not stated. The label's START_TIME and STOP_TIME (2009-07-13 to 2013-07-18) are the span of the LOLA altimetry, not the averaging period. The 2011 paper's abstract also describes runs over several 18.6-year cycles at 6 h steps; the product page says the maps average hourly steps.
   - Observer: on the lunar surface (the 2014 release notes, of the earlier 240 m maps); the product page leaves height above the surface to further analysis.
   - Terrain and grid: LDEM_85S_060M, 60 m per pixel, polar stereographic on the 1737.4 km sphere, true scale at the pole, 5058 × 5058 pixels, pixel-registered.
   - Frame: MEAN EARTH/POLAR AXIS OF DE421 (label). Our MOON_ME is MOON_ME_DE440_ME421, which NAIF's frame kernel aligns with it to 5.8e-7 rad, about 1 m on the ground, over 1900 to 2051; the map's ephemeris is not stated (ours is DE440).
   - Method: horizon elevations in 720 directions (0.5°) and the disc cut by a straight skyline at its centre's direction, with limb darkening (as Barker et al. 2021, Planet. Space Sci. 203, 105119, describe it).

   We work each covered site out one change at a time: **app** (2 m mast, LOLA 20/80/240 m, hourly 2024-2036, any part of the disc), **surface** (the same from the ground), **cycle** (and hourly over 18.6 years from 2024-01-01, 6,798 days), **matched** (and on 60 m posts made from 3 × 3 means of LDEM_85S_20M, aligned with the map's pixel centres, then LOLA 80 m where they end and 240 m from 100 to 220 km, worked out at the map's four pixel centres around the site and combined with the map's bilinear weights). The gap is matched minus map, in percentage points. For Earth we give the app's rule (Earth's centre above the skyline) and the map's (any part of a 6,371 km disc).

   Criteria: the median gap over the covered sites is 5 points or less for the Sun map and for the Earth map; a site agrees within 5 points. Results (generated 2026-11-14): 15 of the 18 polar sites are on the map (IM-1, Nobile Rim 2 and the peak near Cabeus B lie outside its square, which reaches 85° S on every side). Sunlight: median 0.9 points, 14 of 15 within 5 and 12 within 2. Earth in view: median 0.2 points, 14 of 15 within 5. The one site beyond 5 points is the pole itself (+7.0 for the Sun, +6.1 for Earth): one of its four map pixels reads 0 % beside neighbours at 27 to 30 % (within about 1 km of the pole the maps have zero pixels among lit ones), and without that pixel the gaps are −0.3 and +1.2 points. The app's own numbers sit a median 2.6 points from the Sun map (worst +10.8 at the Shackleton rim high point) and 4.1 points from the Earth map, because of what the app does differently on purpose: the 2 m mast raises the sunlit share by a median 1.4 points (19 points on the connecting ridge's 20 m terrain), finer terrain resolves narrow peaks and slopes the 60 m map smooths, and the Earth-centre rule counts fewer hours than any part of the disc. Using 2024-2036 (13 years) instead of the full cycle moves the shares by at most 0.3 points. The assumptions we could not match are listed in `evidence.unmatched`: the averaging dates, the map's own DEM (no longer archived), how far its skylines reach beyond its grid, its ephemeris (the frames themselves agree to about 1 m), the march's steps, limb darkening (it matters only for the mean disc fraction), Earth's radius, and the label's own slips (the Earth label gives MINIMUM_LATITUDE 90 and says "sunlit"; the 2016 release label gave CENTER_LONGITUDE 180 and INT32).

   Limits: both sides are models built from LOLA altimetry with similar methods, so errors they share cancel; this is agreement between two models, not a ground observation (Mazarico et al. 2011 compared their model with LROC WAC images). Averages over 18.6 years can hide errors in the hour of single sunrises and sunsets.

3. **Power sums** (`power.json`, a check). Establishes that the panel and battery arithmetic gives the answers worked out by hand formula, in the pipeline's model (`power.py`) and in the app's engine (`power.ts`, run in the browser on the Accuracy page). Criteria: every worked case within 1e-6 W and 1e-6 Wh, with brownout hours and the first brownout hour exactly equal. Coverage: nine worked cases (37 hours, all four panel models), among them full sunshine at 1 AU (0.3 × 4 m² × 1361 W/m² = 1633.2 W), half the disc, 0.983 AU, a vertical panel 60° off the Sun, the Sun behind a vertical panel, a flat panel at 3°, the Sun 1° below the flat horizon, a night on a full battery and a recharge from half full; and the five recorded runs in `data/fixtures/power/` (two sites, 6,480 hours), which the pipeline reruns against its record and `web/src/lib/engine/power.test.ts` compares with the app's engine. Limits: it checks the sums, not hardware (no panel heating, dust, ageing, wiring or battery losses, heaters or real loads); the worked answers use the same formulas, so a wrong formula would pass.

4. **Mission context** (`backcasts.json`, not a check: no criteria and no pass mark). Our predicted local sunset after each CLPS landing (LOLA skyline, or a flat horizon for Blue Ghost's mare site) beside the end its operator reported: Blue Ghost Mission 1 ended 2.4 h after our predicted sunset (Firefly reported about 5 h after the sunset it saw, so our flat-horizon sunset is about 2.5 h late there); IM-1 ended 67 h before it, tipped over with its panels away from the low Sun; IM-2 299 h before it, on its side in a crater. It can tell whether our sunset comes before or after a reported end when a lander ran into the night, and that the Sun was still up when IM-1 and IM-2 ended. It cannot tell the hour of sunset (mission ends are reported events, not observed sunsets, and two have no published hour: 00:00 and 12:00 UTC are used), whether a battery lasts the night, or how often the forecast is right (one case is not a sample).

Error budget: see the next section, which replaces the earlier estimate ("event timing ±15–60 minutes near the pole") with measured margins.

## Uncertainty budget (review P1, November 2026)

`uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli uncertainty` writes `data/uncertainty/budget.json` (synced to the app by `sync-local`) and `data/uncertainty/skylines/<site>.json`. For every featured site (the 21 polar sites with a mask and Blue Ghost Mission 1), over the 730 days from 2025-01-01, it measures how far each of the app's event times can be off, from three sources kept apart:

- **Sampling.** The app reads the sky once an hour (the ephemeris table's rows at the top of each hour) and names an event at the first hourly sample in its new state. The pipeline finds the same events every minute from SPICE itself (DE440 `de440s.bsp`, `MOON_ME`, `LT+S`, observer at the Moon's centre, exactly as the table was made, so no interpolation is involved), with the same skyline, the same Sun-disk test (any part of the Sun above the skyline) and the same battery walk at a one-minute step. A Sun or Earth crossing is refined linearly between the two minutes that bracket it; battery times are to the minute. At the hours themselves the one-minute flags agree with the hourly ones at every sample (agreement 1.0 at all 19 sites), and the table's float32 rows sit within 3e-6° of SPICE. Why this is sound: the comparison changes only the time step, so its offsets are the sampling error and nothing else. Over 2,186 Sun and Earth crossings the hourly time is later than the one-minute time by 0 to 1 h (largest 0.998 h, as it must be); over 1,301 battery events the hourly walk's time is within 1 h of the one-minute walk's. Short episodes the hourly series can skip are counted per site (`short_episodes`): Mons Mouton has five sunlit spells under an hour in two years (2.8 h in all).
- **Terrain.** Each polar site's skyline is traced again through PGDA product 90's adjusted LOLA models (Barker et al. 2023): the 20 m model within 6 km of the sites south of 87.55° S, the 80 m model to 100 km and the 240 m model to 220 km, with the app's bands, march, fallback bands and 2 m mast. A control run of the same code on the app's own LOLA grids reproduces the stored masks to 0.0005°. The adjusted skyline is then raised and lowered by one stated height error (the `ERR` maps at the point that makes the skyline and at the site, combined in quadrature, over the distance). The one-minute events under those three skylines are compared with the events under the app's skyline: the terrain spread of an event is its largest shift; an event one of those skylines does not have at all is counted as lost, not as a shift. Since November the terrain spread also takes the site's terrain versions (next section): where the 95th percentile of an event's shifts over the versions that keep it is larger, it stands in place of the shift under the three skylines; an event most versions lose widens nothing, and the events more than one version in twenty loses are counted beside the lost ones. The versions only ever widen a margin. In 95 % of directions the adjusted skyline lies within 0.01° to 0.99° of ours, site by site; the largest gaps come where the skyline is the ground 100 to 150 m away, so a metre or two of height moves it by most of a degree (at the Shackleton rim summit the adjusted model puts the summit itself 1.7 m lower, and the gap reaches 1.2°).
- **Ephemeris.** The largest gap to JPL Horizons (`data/validation/horizons.json`: 2.2e-6° in height and 2.6e-6° in direction for the Sun, 2.7e-5° and 1.3e-6° for Earth, over 300 rows), added together and divided by the rate of the body's height above the skyline at each one-minute crossing. The 95th percentile is under 4 s at every site (largest single crossing 9 s).

**The margin.** For each site and event the supported plus-or-minus is the sum of the three 95th percentiles of the size of each error (sampling, terrain, ephemeris), rounded up to the next half hour for display. They are added, not root-summed, because the sampling error is one-sided and the terrain spread is not symmetric. A flat-horizon site (Blue Ghost) carries a stated 2.5 h terrain allowance in place of a measurement: Firefly's report puts the sunset Blue Ghost saw about 2.5 h before our flat-horizon sunset (`backcasts.json`). A point that is not in the catalogue gets the largest margin found at the featured polar sites (`pooled.polar.*.largest_shown_h`), and says so.

What the numbers mean: near the pole the terrain data, not the hourly step or the ephemeris, sets the margin, because the Sun and Earth cross the skyline at a few hundredths of a degree an hour, so a skyline a tenth of a degree higher moves an event by hours. The totals move too: at the Shackleton rim summit the adjusted maps give 86.2 % sunlight against our 90.7 % and a longest night of 126.7 h against our 62.5 h over the same two years; at the peak near Shackleton the longest night shortens from 117 days to 58. What they assume: the default lander for battery events (4 m² upright panel turning to the Sun, 30 %, 200 W, 2 kWh), a 2 m mast and Earth counted in view whenever its centre clears the skyline. What they do not establish: the product 90 models are not the truth either (their own error is part of the spread, as one standard error, not a bound); hills smaller than the models resolve, the skyline between its 720 directions and the lander's own height, tilt and placement are not in the budget.

**The ground at each site** (`terrain_quality`, from windows of the 20 m and 50 m product 90 maps around each site): the stated height error (0.16 to 2.6 m; Nobile Rim 2's 2.6 m with a 220 m effective resolution marks sparse LOLA tracks there), the slope and its error, the effective resolution, the roughness at a 100 m baseline and the nearest permanently shadowed pixel within 10 km (`LPSR_80S_20MPP_ADJ`). The app shows it under the site's description on Explore (Where on the Moon), in the brief, and in a table on the Accuracy page.

**The power model's scope**, as the app states it beside the lander, in All numbers and in the brief: a constant load, a constant panel efficiency, an ideal battery (no loss to cold or age) and one height, the mast's, for both the panel and the radio. The figures count electrical energy only; they do not show whether the lander stays warm enough through the night or starts again after it, and the verdict says power comes back only "if the lander starts again after the cold".

## Terrain versions: an error model fitted to two generations of maps (November 2026)

Steps 1 and 2 of the plan in `docs/ml-feasibility.md`: fit an error model to the two terrain generations, then draw versions of the ground from it and trace each to a skyline. `uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli error-model` writes `data/ensemble/error_model.json` (36 s); `uv run python -m lunar_pipeline.cli ensemble` writes `data/ensemble/<site>.json` for the 21 polar catalogue sites and `data/ensemble/index.json` (about 22 minutes on one core with other jobs running: about 1 s a version for the fields and 0.5 s for the march); `sync-local` copies them into the app. In all 1.38 MB: 32 versions of 720 directions per site as base64 int16 thousandths of a degree (63.8 kB a site, 1,339,517 bytes for the 21), the index (4.7 kB) and the model with its semivariograms and cells (25.7 kB).

**The data.** The app's LOLA grids at PDS (`ldem_875s_20m`, `ldem_80s_80m`, `ldem_60s_240m`) minus PGDA product 90's adjusted models (Barker et al. 2023) on the same pixels (the two generations share their polar stereographic lattices exactly), less the median over each window, since a common offset moves no skyline: 6.5 km squares at 20 m round the 8 sites south of 87.55° S (product 90's cached 20 m windows), 30 km squares at 80 m round the 18 sites the 80 m grids cover, and 90 km squares at 240 m round all 21; at 80 and 240 m each pixel counts once where squares overlap (5.6 and 4.5 million pixels), at 20 m each window counts its own (3.4 million). Beside them, product 90's stated one-sigma error (`ERR`) on the same pixels and its slope.

**The fit.** The size: the mean square difference in cells of slope (2.5° to 10° bins) by stated error (bins at its quantiles), fitted by non-negative weighted least squares with sigma² = a² + (b tan slope)² + (c ERR)², where b tan slope is what a horizontal shift between the models does on a slope and c ERR ties the error to the sparse tracks product 90 already flags. The correlation length: the semivariogram of the differences divided by that sigma, worked out over every pair of pixels in each window with Fourier transforms and pooled over the windows, fitted with s (1 − exp(−h / L)) at lags up to 1 km.

What the fit says. The two generations, built from the same laser shots, differ by about 1.4 m (2 m on the 240 m grid), 3 to 29 times the error product 90 states, and most where the ground is steep: on the 80 m grid the RMS difference grows from 0.6 m below 2.5° of slope to 3.0 m above 25° (0.5 to 3.2 m on the 20 m grid), as a horizontal shift of about 8 m between the models, in any direction, would make it. They are correlated over less than two pixels (32, 58 and 131 m): neighbouring points of the ground are off by different amounts, not the hundreds of metres to kilometres the feasibility study's prototype tried. Past that the semivariogram climbs only slowly, by about 5 % of its sill out to 20 km (the slow part, a few tenths of a metre). The fitted Gaussian holds about the right share within two sigmas (95 to 96 % against 95.4 %) but more within one (71 to 79 % against 68.3 %): the differences are heavier-tailed than a Gaussian. At local summits of the app's grids the differences are much larger (5.8 m RMS on the 20 m grid, 1.3 m higher on average), where at summits of product 90's they are not (0.5 m): the older grids have spikes, and a point chosen as the highest of the older grid tends to sit on one.

**The versions.** Each is product 90's adjusted model plus sigma(x) z(x) on every grid the site's skyline is traced through (the 20 m window within 6 km of the sites south of 87.55° S, the 80 m model to 100 km, the 240 m model to 220 km, each cropped to what its band reaches): sigma from the fit at that grid's resolution with product 90's slope and stated error there, z a unit Gaussian field with the fitted exponential correlation, drawn by circulant embedding (exact on the periodic grid, which is far wider than the length), afresh for every version and grid, from a seed fixed per site (`[20261115, crc32(site id)]`). The site's height is read from the version it stands on, so its own error moves every direction together. Each version is traced with the pipeline's own march (`uncertainty.horizon_profile`) through the app's bands and fallback bands from the 2 m mast, exactly as the budget traces product 90's skyline; with no error the code reproduces the budget's stored adjusted skylines to 0.0005°. North of 80° S (Chandrayaan-3, Luna 25's target, the centre of Schrödinger) only the 240 m grids reach, as for the app's own skylines there.

What the versions show (from `index.json`). The skyline's spread over the versions (its standard deviation in each direction, median over directions) runs from 0.001° (de Gerlache rim summit, whose skyline is ground 78 km away) to 0.98° (Nobile Rim 2, whose sparse tracks product 90 states as 2.3 m of error at the site), and is 1.6 to 15 times the skyline error the budget derives from product 90's stated error (median over directions; over 500 times at the de Gerlache rim summit, whose stated skyline error is a few ten-thousandths of a degree). Their middle sits above product 90's skyline by under 0.05° in the median direction at every site but two (0.21° at the peak near Cabeus B and 0.35° at Nobile Rim 2, where the errors are largest): the highest point of a noisy profile is on average higher than that of the smooth one. Calibration: if the model captured how the two generations differ, the app's own skyline (the older generation) would fall within the versions' middle 90 % in about 90 % of directions. It does at the median site (92 %; 75 to 100 % at 17 of the 21 sites, both skylines read at the stored thousandth of a degree), but not at four: the peak near Shackleton (8 %), the Shackleton rim summit (18 %), Slater Plain (43 %) and IM-1 (63 %). There it is the site's own height: the older grids put the peak near Shackleton 1.8 m and Slater Plain 2.0 m lower than product 90 does, the rim summit 1.7 m and IM-1 1.4 m higher, while the versions move these four sites by 0.3 to 1.1 m (one standard deviation). So the versions describe how far the ground around NASA's adjusted map may be off where it is like most ground; at a summit picked from the older grid, or a peak the older grid flattened, the two maps disagree by more, and the budget's margins (which include the step from the app's skyline to product 90's) remain the wider statement.

**What it cannot capture.** Errors both generations share: both come from the same LOLA laser shots, so a hill neither resolves, or a track error both inherit, is absent from the differences and from the versions. The slow part of the differences, a few tenths of a metre over tens of kilometres, which moves a site and its skyline together and is left out (the budget's raised and lowered skylines stand in for it). The differences' heavier-than-Gaussian tails and their alignment along laser tracks (the model is isotropic). Anything under one pixel. Whether product 90 or the older grids is nearer the truth: the differences are the older grids' error if product 90 is right, and used as product 90's own error the model is cautious, several times the error it states. And the versions say nothing about the lander's own height, tilt and placement, or about the battery's temperature; a share of versions is not the chance that a landing works.

**In the budget.** The fitted error moves the skyline several times as far as the stated error does, so the budget's terrain part now takes the versions too (previous section). Of the 114 margins at the 18 sites the budget measured before, 68 grew and none shrank: by half an hour to a few hours at most sites (Connecting Ridge's sunset ±3.5 to ±4.5 h and its battery ±3.5 to ±6.5 h; IM-2 Athena's sunset ±2.0 to ±3.5 h), and by many hours where the Sun or Earth grazes ground the versions move most (Nobile Rim 2's sunset ±3.0 to ±22.0 h, the peak near Cabeus B's Earth setting ±3.0 to ±18.5 h, Malapert Massif's sunrise ±7.0 to ±18.0 h, the Shackleton rim summit's sunset ±10.5 to ±21.0 h). The largest margin at the polar sites, which stands for a point the budget has not measured, went from 17 to 23 h to 24 to 24.5 h, close to the 24 h within which an event is matched at all. Chandrayaan-3, Luna 25's target and the centre of Schrödinger, north of 80° S, are measured for the first time (sunset ±1.5, ±2.0 and ±5.5 h; Earth never sets at the first two and never rises at the third) and have no ground figures, since product 90's 20 m and 50 m maps end at 80° S. Over the two years the versions give Connecting Ridge a longest night of 303 to 487 h (5th to 95th percentile; 486 h on the app's skyline), the Shackleton rim summit 91 to 267 h (62.5 h on the app's skyline, below the 5th percentile of the versions) and IM-2 Athena 411 to 412 h (411 h).

**In the app.** For the chosen landing and period, a worker (`web/src/workers/ensemble.worker.ts`, pure modules only) runs the app's own engine over each version's skyline, reading where the Sun and Earth are from the period's own hourly series (each version only changes what the hills cover): the range of time with power, first sunset, longest night and contact with Earth while powered, with the app's own figure beside each and a word when it lies outside every version, and how many versions still meet the requirements set or, with none, keep the verdict (power for the whole period or not). Explore shows it in a closed fold, "Terrain versions", beside "Times ±N h"; Compare in a row per scenario; the brief in a printed part under how sure the times are; the Accuracy page in a part of its own. None of it changes a figure of the verdict, which stays that of the app's own skyline. Versions exist for the 21 polar catalogue sites from the 2 m mast; a point picked on the map, or another mast height, says it has none.

**Ranking by how often a choice holds.** Objectives' ranking (`web/src/lib/plan/rank.ts`) takes it as an optional requirement, "Holds in at least N % of terrain versions" (`Requirements.minHoldPct`), not as a criterion of its own after the requirements. A version holds when it meets the other requirements set, or, with none set, when power lasts the whole stay. Set, it counts as any requirement: a choice that holds in fewer versions fails it and ranks below every choice that meets them all, and its check reads "29 of 32 versions (91 %)". It is a requirement and not a default because a share of versions rests on the error model's assumptions and is not a probability: unless a planner asks for it, the ranking and the better date stay exactly as worked out on the app's own skyline, and when they ask, the reason for any change is a check they can read. The worker counts, for every sunlit landing hour of the period, the versions that hold for a stay as long as the period; Explore's landing times in the period are checked against it. Places nearby and the 12-month search do not check it (the box says so).

## Kinds of point, the far side and the equator (P1)

- Every catalogue site says what kind of point it is (`kind` in `data/sites.json`, worded by `web/src/lib/data/kind.ts`): a surveyed point (lander hardware found in orbital images), a target (chosen in advance: Luna 25's, IM-3's, a study's candidate), the rough centre of a region (the Artemis III regions read off NASA's map, ±5 km; Schrödinger basin for CP-12, ±158 km), a derived point (the highest LOLA height in a ring round a crater, never checked as a safe place to land) or a reference point (the pole, crater centres). The Sites page, Explore, Compare and the brief show it.
- Far side: Chang'e 4, Chang'e 6 and the centre of Schrödinger basin never see Earth: over every hour of 2024 to 2036 Earth's centre stays at least 2.3° below a flat horizon there (37° and 33° for the Chang'e sites), so even the top of its disc never shows. The app says Earth never rises there, rather than counting hours out of view; Chang'e 4 and 6 talked to Earth through China's Queqiao relay satellites.
- Near the equator the Sun rises and sets once a lunar day: about 15 days of light, then 15 of night, with Earth always in view on the near side. The default lander's upright panel turns to the Sun but catches the light at the cosine of its height, so at Apollo 11, 0.7° from the equator, its output falls below the 200 W load for hours around noon, with the Sun almost overhead, and the battery runs out then; the verdict says the Sun is too high for the panel, not that there is too little sunlight. A flat panel or one that follows the Sun keeps going.
- Landings before 2024 (Apollo, Chang'e 3 to 5, Chandrayaan-3) keep their date only: the table starts in 2024, so their figures are for the period chosen and no day before it is offered. Backcasts take only landings inside the table that have a reported end (IM-1, IM-2, Blue Ghost Mission 1).
